The Physical Spectroscope & Digital Analysis
🔬 Physical Spectroscope

Your spectroscope is built from a cardboard tube (or box) with a narrow entrance slit at one end and an old CD/DVD at the other. The microscopic grooves on the CD surface act as a diffraction grating — they split incoming white light into its component wavelengths by constructive and destructive interference. Different wavelengths exit at slightly different angles, spreading the light into a rainbow (spectrum). Each colour corresponds to a particular wavelength range: violet (~400 nm) bends the most, red (~700 nm) the least.

💻 Digital Image Analysis

When you photograph the spectrum and upload the image here, the app reads every pixel of the selected region using the HTML Canvas API. For each vertical column of pixels, it averages the red, green, and blue channel values across the selected height. It then computes a relative brightness value using the standard luminance formula Y = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B (ITU-R BT.709). This brightness profile is normalised to 0–100 and plotted against horizontal pixel position. Peaks in this profile correspond to regions of the spectrum where your light source emits more intensely — but remember, the image is affected by camera response, exposure, and optical geometry.

⚠ Scientific Limitations & Caveats
Results represent relative image brightness, not absolute physical light intensity.
Camera response curves, exposure settings, and JPEG compression affect channel intensities.
CD/DVD groove spacing produces non-linear wavelength dispersion; pixel position ≠ wavelength without calibration.
Reflections, ambient light, and spectroscope alignment affect the measured spectrum shape.
Detected peaks may be artefacts of image noise or reflections, not actual spectral lines.
Methodology
1. IMAGE LOADING: Photograph uploaded and decoded into an HTML <canvas> element. No data is sent to any server; all processing is in-browser (client-side only). 2. REGION SELECTION: User selects a horizontal band (adjustable top % and height %) corresponding to the visible spectrum in the photograph. 3. PIXEL EXTRACTION: For each vertical column x in the selected region, all pixel RGBA values at rows y ∈ [y_top, y_top+height] are read using CanvasRenderingContext2D.getImageData(). 4. CHANNEL AVERAGING: For each column x: R(x) = mean of red channel across all rows in the band G(x) = mean of green channel across all rows in the band B(x) = mean of blue channel across all rows in the band 5. LUMINANCE FORMULA (ITU-R BT.709): Y(x) = 0.2126 × R(x) + 0.7152 × G(x) + 0.0722 × B(x) This weights the channels by the human eye's sensitivity to each colour. 6. NORMALISATION: Y values are scaled linearly so that the maximum value in the profile equals 100 and the minimum equals 0. 7. PEAK DETECTION: Local maxima are identified where: - The value exceeds the user-set threshold (default: 15 above baseline). - Adjacent peaks are separated by at least 8 pixel columns. - Peak prominence is checked to reduce noise detections. 8. CALIBRATION (optional): If ≥2 calibration points are provided, a linear regression maps pixel position x to wavelength λ (nm). The fitted equation λ = a·x + b is displayed. Nonlinearity is noted.

Physics & Diffraction Theory

Core diffraction grating principles, dispersion equations, pixel analysis methodology, and experimental limitations.

What is a Spectroscope?

A spectroscope is an optical instrument that separates light into its constituent wavelengths (its spectrum). It uses either a prism or a diffraction grating to spread different wavelengths at different angles. Our DIY version uses a cardboard tube with a narrow entrance slit and an old CD/DVD as the dispersing element. When you look through (or photograph) the CD end while illuminating the slit, you see the rainbow spectrum of the light source.

How a CD/DVD Separates Light

A CD has about 1,600 grooves per millimetre etched into its polycarbonate surface — a diffraction grating. When light strikes these grooves, each groove acts as a secondary wave source. For a particular wavelength λ, constructive interference occurs at angles θ satisfying the grating equation d sin θ = mλ, where d is the groove spacing and m is the diffraction order (usually m = 1 for the first-order spectrum). Since sin θ differs for each λ, different colours fan out at different angles, producing the visible spectrum.

Diffraction & Interference

Diffraction is the bending and spreading of waves when they pass through a narrow opening or around an obstacle. Interference is what happens when two or more waves overlap — they add up (constructive) where crests meet, or cancel (destructive) where a crest meets a trough. The grating produces a bright spot at angles where contributions from all grooves arrive exactly in phase (path difference = whole number of wavelengths). This is why specific colours appear at specific angles.

Types of Spectra

Continuous spectrum: A smooth, unbroken rainbow of all visible wavelengths. Produced by hot incandescent solids (e.g., an old filament bulb).

Line spectrum (emission): Bright coloured lines on a dark background. Specific wavelengths only, unique to each element (e.g., sodium's yellow doublet). Produced by excited low-pressure gas (e.g., neon signs, CFL tubes at lower pressure).

Band spectrum: Groups of closely spaced lines (molecular spectra). Produced by molecules rather than individual atoms.

From Image Pixels to Data

Every digital photograph stores colour as a grid of pixels. Each pixel has three 8-bit channel values: Red (0–255), Green (0–255), Blue (0–255). The HTML Canvas API lets JavaScript read every pixel's RGB values directly. By averaging all pixel rows in the selected band for each column, we get a brightness profile across the spectrum. The luminance formula Y = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B mimics the human eye's response, giving more weight to green (where our eyes are most sensitive).

Wavelength Calibration

To convert pixel positions to wavelengths, you need to identify at least two known spectral features and their pixel positions in the image. For example, if you observe sodium's yellow doublet at ~589 nm and mercury's green line at ~546 nm, you can fit a linear equation λ = a·x + b. This converts any pixel x to an approximate wavelength λ. Accuracy depends on how well your calibration points are identified and how linear the dispersion actually is (the CD grating can introduce nonlinear effects).

Limitations & Experimental Errors

Geometric distortion: CD grooves are circular; a flat photograph introduces mapping errors. Camera effects: Sensors saturate, auto-white-balance modifies colour ratios, JPEG compression loses detail. Alignment: If the slit is not perpendicular to the grating grooves, the spectrum tilts. Ambient light: Background illumination adds a constant brightness offset. Quantisation: 8-bit channel depth (256 levels) limits precision. These limitations mean our results are qualitative, not primary measurement standards.
